North Korea Publishes Photos: Why Death Rumours Persist

North Korea publishes photos of Kim Jong Un to quell health fears. Learn why death rumours persist and get the real facts. Stay informed on the latest news.

QM
Quillmatick Staff October 21, 2026
6 min read

Verification Guidelines

  • Cross-verify any claim about North Korean leadership with official state media releases; the Rodong Sinmun photos provide direct visual evidence.
  • Scrutinize source provenance; activists such as Wanjun Xie have a documented history of circulating unverified stories, therefore flag their reports for additional fact-checking.
  • Monitor succession indicators—public appearances with designated heirs or changes in entourage—because irregularities often signal genuine shifts rather than speculation.
  • Triangulate events using satellite imagery, diplomatic communications, and reputable news agencies; consequently, you can confirm developments when on-the-ground verification is impossible.
  • Log recurring rumor cycles (e.g., health scares, death rumors) to anticipate future misinformation spikes, enabling proactive communication strategies.

State Media Releases Photos Showing Kim Jong Un Healthy

The official press released new images that directly contradict death rumors.

  • Kim Jong Un appears in a Rodong Sinmun photo tour of a clothing and footwear exhibition, accompanied by his daughter Kim Ju Ae and his usual entourage.
  • The photos surfaced only hours after an online claim alleged that the leader had been rushed to Beijing in a coma.

Why the timing matters: the state’s rapid visual response suggests a deliberate effort to counter misinformation.

  • The images show Kim Jong Un examining displays, indicating he is active and mobile.
  • Although the exact timestamp of the photos is unclear, the visual evidence outweighs unsubstantiated reports.

Origin of the Latest Death Rumours

The rumor originated from an exile activist.

  • Wanjun Xie, self-identified chairman of the banned Chinese Democratic Party, posted the claim that Kim Jong Un suffered a severe brain haemorrhage and was transferred to Beijing for treatment.
  • Xie further alleged that the leader died and that the body was secretly returned to North Korea.

Xie’s History of Unverified Claims

  • Previously circulated false reports about the death of Chinese leader Xi Jinping.
  • Frequently issues sensational statements without evidence, therefore his claims require rigorous verification.

Frequently Asked Questions

** The Rodong Sinmun images show him actively touring an exhibition, which directly contradicts the death claim.

Who initiated the recent death rumours? The rumors were first posted by Wanjun Xie, an exile activist linked to the banned Chinese Democratic Party.

How can analysts differentiate fact from rumor in such cases? By applying the verification guidelines above—cross-checking with official media, triangulating with satellite data, and tracking the source’s credibility.

Xie’s pattern mirrors a long history of false alarms involving regional strongmen. Is this journalism or just a bid for relevance?

He may simply have been seeking attention.

Or he may have wanted to embarrass Beijing by highlighting its ties to Pyongyang, although those are well-established for the world to see.

But why does the world keep biting?

Why rumours keep resurfacing

These rumors don't survive on evidence; they survive on a vacuum.

For those who watch North Korea closely, this kind of rumour has become almost routine.

In one of the world's most secretive states, facts are often difficult to verify, creating fertile ground for speculation.

Succession uncertainty

The Kim dynasty has held power for three generations, but the road ahead looks blurrier than ever.

North Korea has been ruled by the Kim family for three generations, but what happens after Kim Jong Un remains less certain than it was for his predecessors.

The old way was a slow burn.

Kim Il Sung spent decades preparing his son, Kim Jong Il, for power.

By the time Kim Il Sung died in 1994, the succession plan was firmly established.

Kim Jong Il's transition was relatively smooth.

Kim Jong Un, however, was a sprint.

Kim Jong Un's rise was faster and more precarious.

After Kim Jong Il suffered a stroke in 2008, his youngest son was rapidly promoted through the ranks.

By the time his father died in 2011, he had been publicly identified as the future leader.

Even so, Kim Jong Un spent years consolidating power, purging rivals and eliminating perceived threats.

Now, the spotlight is shifting toward a child.

Today, South Korea's intelligence agency believes he may be preparing his daughter as a successor.

She has increasingly appeared at high-profile events and military displays.

But she is thought to be only 13 years old.

North Korea has never been led by a woman, and she has not been formally designated as heir.

Her youth would also leave her vulnerable in any power struggle.

If the heir is a child, any hint of the father's illness becomes a geopolitical crisis.

All of this uncertainty helps explain why rumours about Kim's health attract so much attention.

Health plausibility

The rumors feel plausible because the risk factors are real.

Kim is overweight and a heavy smoker.

Seoul doesn't hide its concerns.

South Korea's National Intelligence Service has repeatedly warned that his lifestyle puts him at higher risk of cardiovascular disease.

Heart problems and strokes also run in the Kim family.

If Kim falls, the world shakes.

If anything were to happen to Kim, the stakes are high for neighbouring countries and the wider international community.

Any sudden leadership crisis raises difficult questions about control of North Korea's nuclear weapons and the prospect of political instability.

Historical false alarms

We've been here before.

The rumour also echoes previous false alarms.

In 2020, a high-profile North Korean defector claimed he was "99% certain" Kim had died following cardiovascular surgery.

Not long afterwards, Kim appeared in public and the claim unravelled.

Despite the failures, the world stays on edge because the cost of being wrong about a death is low, but the cost of being wrong about a collapse is catastrophic.

International stakes of a potential leadership crisis

There would probably be signs beyond North Korea's borders if there were a sudden collapse in Kim's health.

China could tighten security along its frontier to prepare for potential instability or refugee flows.

Activity around North Korean diplomatic missions might also increase.

Defectors in South Korea often receive information through informal networks along the Chinese border.

None of this has happened.

Seoul is equally skeptical.

South Korean intelligence officials, meanwhile, told Yonhap they had heard nothing to support the reports.

And Washington is always watching.

These are questions that concern not only Beijing and Seoul but also Washington, which maintains around 28,000 troops in South Korea.

Information environment and regime behavior

North Korea is an information black hole. That makes it the perfect canvas for fiction.

Because North Korea remains an information black hole, it is also an easy target for false reports.

Only a tiny circle has regular access to its leader.

Even intelligence agencies struggle to obtain reliable information, and much of what emerges is heavily caveated as unverified or highly likely rather than confirmed fact.

The regime might even be playing along.

The regime itself is also suspected of deliberately encouraging confusion, leaking misleading information to obscure what is really happening inside the country.

In this environment, a claim from a dissident in the US carries almost no weight.

So it is hard to believe that Wanjun Xie had somehow stumbled across such significant news, when it had eluded those whose job it is to stay across it.

Whatever his motive, Xie is far from the first person to exploit the uncertainty surrounding North Korea's leadership.
