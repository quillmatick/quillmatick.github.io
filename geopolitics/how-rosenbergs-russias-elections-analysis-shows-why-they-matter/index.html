Key takeaways

  • Managed democracy concentrates power in the president; electoral analysis must track Kremlin-directed narratives that suppress competition.
  • The election functions as a domestic legitimacy test for the war; any deviation from a pro-Putin outcome may indicate changing public sentiment.

Managed Democracy and the Expectation of No Surprises

Russia’s managed democracy grants the president extensive authority while limiting genuine political competition. **

Definition and Implications

  • The president controls the agenda; opposition parties operate within narrowly defined parameters.
  • Legislative outcomes routinely reaffirm the existing power hierarchy, leaving little room for unexpected results.

The Stakes of the 2024 Duma Election: Legitimacy and Veteran Promotion

**

  • Veteran influx may shift legislative priorities toward military spending and security legislation.
  • Voter turnout and candidate performance will be scrutinized for signs of public fatigue or dissent.

Campaign Oddities and State Media Presentation

State-run media have replaced substantive debate with spectacle. However, these tactics expose the regime’s reliance on image over policy.

  • Empty lecterns on televised debates signal a lack of genuine opposition.

Parliamentary Unity Behind the “Special Military Operation”

  • Uniform legislative support eliminates formal dissent, consolidating Kremlin control over foreign-policy decisions.
  • The legal framework legitimizes ongoing military actions domestically and internationally.

Suppression of Opposition: Yabloko’s Exclusion and Candidate Harassment

Opposition parties face systematic barriers. Additionally, Yabloko’s exclusion from the ballot illustrates the broader strategy of marginalizing dissent.

  • Candidates experience intimidation, legal obstacles, and media blackout.
  • These tactics ensure that only Kremlin-approved voices appear on the ballot.

Crackdown on Election Monitoring and Civil Society

Independent observers encounter increasing restrictions. **

  • Monitoring missions require cumbersome permits, limiting real-time oversight.
  • Civil-society groups report harassment, reducing their capacity to document abuses.

Ground-Level Perspectives: Daily Concerns in Dmitrov

Residents of Dmitrov express practical worries that differ from elite rhetoric. Consequently, local sentiment provides early warning signs of broader discontent.

  • Economic strain, utility shortages, and war-related inflation dominate household concerns.
  • Community forums reveal skepticism toward official turnout figures.

How Russian Elections Differ from Western Democracies

Russian elections prioritize regime stability over competitive choice. In contrast, Western democracies emphasize pluralism, transparent campaigning, and independent oversight.

Feature Russia Typical Western Democracy
Power concentration President dominates Power dispersed among branches
Opposition access Heavily restricted Open ballot access

Election monitoring Limited, often obstructed Independent, international observers

This weekend’s election is set to deliver another parliament dominated by the party of power, United Russia, and a house of MPs fiercely loyal to Vladimir Putin. This is the first parliamentary election here since Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine.** The Kremlin will use it to claim that most Russians back the president and the war, as well as to promote the political careers of soldiers back from Ukraine: officials say nearly two hundred war veterans are taking part in the election.

Campaign Oddities and State Media Presentation

The election campaign has offered a rare glimpse into Russian society four-and-a-half years into the war. I said no surprises. But there have been some.

Empty Lecterns on State TV

“Good evening and welcome to tonight's campaign speeches by candidates for Duma Electoral District 158,” said the anchor on state TV. “So, it's goodbye from me.”

Performative Political Messaging

In the previous Duma, everyone seemed to be singing from the same hymn sheet.

Parliamentary Unity Behind the “Special Military Operation”

Uniform Legislative Support

“All parties in the Duma, every single one, voted in favour of 170 laws to support the special military operation,” Communist Party leader Gennady Zyuganov told me with obvious pride about the last parliament. “In that sense there is 100% agreement in the Duma.”

Role of Systemic Parties

I caught up with Zyuganov on the campaign trail outside Moscow at a bread and biscuit factory. As I watched rows of identical-looking loaves speeding along the conveyer I thought about Russia's parliament.

Suppression of Opposition: Yabloko’s Exclusion and Candidate Harassment

Removal from Party List

One such party – Yabloko – is calling for a ceasefire in Ukraine. Back in July it was the only anti-war party in Russia to be registered for the election. Twelve days later, though, the Supreme Court removed it from the ballot. That was widely interpreted as a sign of Kremlin concern that anti-war messaging could strike a chord with Russian voters.

Continued Individual Campaigns and Detentions

Despite Yabloko's exclusion from the party list, some of its candidates are still running in individual constituency races. Though in recent weeks growing numbers of Yabloko candidates have been detained by police, prosecuted for various offences and barred from the election.

Police Narrative on Political Order

“The police are carrying out a political order. That's no secret,” Yabloko candidate Grigory Grishin tells me. In my opinion, most people in Russia now want peace and Yabloko helped people express this. That's why it's been excluded from the party lists, and why individual candidates are being barred. For now, Grigory remains a candidate. But the police recently came to his flat.

Crackdown on Election Monitoring and Civil Society

Jailing of Independent Monitors

The Russian authorities insist that the Duma elections are transparent and fair. Critics of the vote point to opposition politicians who have been forced into exile or put in prison. Last year one of the leaders of a prominent independent election monitoring group in Russia was jailed for five years.

Ground-Level Perspectives: Daily Concerns in Dmitrov

Fifty miles from Moscow, in the town of Dmitrov, I see little sign of an election. In the town centre there's a memorial to “Heroes of the special military operation”: local people killed fighting in Ukraine. The war is front and centre of people's minds here.

War’s Personal Toll

“We feel it,” says Olga, whose son-in-law is a soldier in the “special operation”. “Because of the people who've gone to fight and the families left behind with sons, husbands, children and grandchildren there. But these elections can't influence what will happen.”

Economic Pressures and Fuel Shortages

Ukrainian drone attacks on Russian oil refineries have sparked widespread fuel shortages, while economic pressures are increasing. “What worries me,” says a barber called Ivan, “is that no matter how much you earn it's not enough. Prices are rising faster than wages. I speak to lots of people in my job. I know how hard-up they are.”

How Russian Elections Differ from Western Democracies

“Western elections and elections in Russia are completely different things,” Grishin explains. At best, they're a way of influencing it, of sending a signal. But power in Russia never changed because of an election. That's how Russia is.
